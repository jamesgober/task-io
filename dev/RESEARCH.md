# task-io - R&D Program

> How task-io finds designs that beat every existing job pipeline and worker pool, and the register of every idea tried. Read after [`PLAN.md`](./PLAN.md) and [`BENCHMARKS.md`](./BENCHMARKS.md). The main R&D round is `0.2.0`; the second round is `0.7.0`; ideas can be added at any time.

---

## 1. Why this exists

Copying an existing job queue gets its numbers. The mandate (PLAN 4.1) is at least 1.20 times the best competitor on every benchmark, so the design has to come from measurement, not habit. This file is where ideas are written down, tested, and either adopted with evidence or dropped with evidence.

---

## 2. Rules

1. **Every idea is an entry in section 4** before any code is written for it, with: id, hypothesis, method (the experiment), expected cost (latency, memory, complexity, security effect), success criterion, status, and evidence.
2. **Experiments are code, not opinions.** Each experiment is a small, self-contained program under `.dev/experiments/<id>/` (private) or a suite in `compare/` (public) that anyone can rerun. The result, the command that produced it, and the host are written in the entry.
3. **Status values:** `proposed`, `running`, `adopted`, `dropped`, `parked`. `adopted` needs a measured win on the success criterion and a decision in PLAN section 14. `dropped` needs the measurement that killed it. `parked` needs the reason and the condition that would revive it.
4. **Security and stability are part of the criterion.** An idea that wins on speed but breaks an invariant in DIRECTIVES section 4 is dropped, or fixed and measured again.
5. **Read before you build.** For each area, read the sources in section 5 and write a short note (what the source does, what its limit is, what we would do differently) into `.dev/research/<area>.md`.
6. **Invention quota.** The `0.2.0` design must adopt at least three capabilities that no benchmarked competitor offers (PLAN D-014). Each one is proven by an experiment, not argued.
7. **New ideas are required, not optional.** The `0.2.0` round adds at least ten new entries to section 4 beyond the ones listed here, from reading, profiling, and failed experiments.
8. **Unsoundness is never an option.** An idea that requires undefined behavior, or a soundness argument nobody can write down, is dropped.
9. **Stay in scope.** Ideas for a general executor, actors, or spawns go to `_strategy/EXECUTOR-SEED/` or `_strategy/SPAWN-IO.md`, not here (PLAN D-015).

---

## 3. Method for one experiment

1. Write the entry (section 4) with the hypothesis and success criterion **before** measuring.
2. Build the smallest prototype that tests the hypothesis and nothing else.
3. Measure it against the current design and the best competitor using the BENCHMARKS protocol, on R1 and R2.
4. Profile it (Windows: WPA / ETW; Linux: `perf stat`, `perf record`, cache-miss and branch-miss counters).
5. Write the verdict, the numbers, and the command into the entry. Adopt, drop, or park.

## 6. Findings

Filled in by each R&D round: the short version of what was learned, with links to the entries.

**2026-10-14, quick trials on R1 (harness check runs, not for gating; full-protocol results replace these):**
- The hand-rolled crossbeam pool is the strongest dispatch baseline so far (about 45 ns per empty job at 4 workers). Several contenders become unstable at 32 workers on 32 logical CPUs (I-30).
- Bounding a queue by slots does not bound latency (I-29).
- Replacing threads on panic is very slow; catching is nearly free (I-31).
- Most contenders lose or abandon queued work at shutdown (I-32).
- Delayed-job lateness on Windows is dominated by timer granularity, about 8 ms (I-33).
- tokio's elastic pool oversubscribes CPU-bound work by about 16x (I-37, I-23).
- apalis's in-memory backend cannot take concurrent submissions (I-38).

**2026-10-19, first prototype (`proto-ring` in `compare/src/proto.rs`), quick W-01 and W-04 on R1 (host shared with other builds, so indicative only):**
- I-01 + I-16: a Vyukov ring with inline job slots ran 1M empty jobs in 111 to 124 ms against 102 to 154 ms for crossbeam-pool at 4 workers and 1 submitter: no clear win yet at low worker counts.
- I-04, first design: waking one worker per published job made the pool 5x slower than crossbeam-pool at 32 workers (988 ms against 209 ms): one `unpark` per job. With at most one searching worker and I-05's short spin before parking, the same run took 109 ms against 201 ms for crossbeam-pool (1.84x), and 139 ms against 161 ms with 32 submitters.
- A lost-wakeup deadlock was found in the searching protocol: a waker could count a worker as searching in the window after it announced idleness, and if that worker's re-check found a job, the count leaked and no one was woken again. Fixed by giving the search back on that path. This is exactly the class of bug the loom models in DIRECTIVES section 2 exist to catch; task-io's version of this protocol is not written without one.
- I-35 is needed: with 32 submitters at 4 workers, the shared ring tail made the prototype no faster than crossbeam-pool.
- I-36: W-04's job (64-byte payload plus 16 bytes of harness state) did not fit the 64-byte inline area, so it was boxed: 340 B effective per queued job against 104 B for the best competitor. The 1.20 target (at most about 87 B effective, start reservation included) means the real slot must hold payload, header, and queue cell in about 86 bytes; a separate sequence word per cell (the prototype's 96-byte cell) does not fit.
- P-02 against apalis on SQLite: after a kill -9 and restart, a worker that reused the crashed worker's name processed nothing for at least 120 s (apalis registers workers by name, and the dead one's registration had not expired); with a unique name per process it resumed in 57 ms with 0 lost jobs and 0 duplicates. task-io's recovery must never wait on an old identity to expire: a restarted pool reclaims its own leases at once (fencing tokens make that safe), and the harness gives apalis the unique-name configuration.

**2026-10-19, fang 0.11 on SQLite, quick runs of P-02, R-01, R-02, and K-01 on R1 (host shared with other builds; the timings are indicative only, the counts are not load-dependent):**
- fang runs jobs twice. It claims a job with a `SELECT` and then a separate unconditional `UPDATE ... SET state = 'in_progress'` (no transaction; the PostgreSQL and MySQL queries add `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`, but the lock ends with the `SELECT` statement). With 32 workers: 13 to 24 duplicate runs in 2,000 R-01 jobs (three runs), 33 to 42 duplicate effects in 2,000 P-02 jobs (I-39).

**2026-10-19, a loom model of the prototype wake protocol (`compare/models`, I-04):**
- The idle bitmap, searching count, and park/unpark protocol of `proto-ring` and `proto-shards`, with a job counter in place of the queue, explored over every interleaving within loom's preemption bound: 2 workers with 2 and 3 jobs (bound 3), 3 workers with 2 jobs (bound 2). No interleaving leaves a published job unrun with every thread parked. 189 s on R2 for the whole suite.
- The same protocol without the lost-wakeup fix (the searching count leaked on the idle re-check) deadlocks in loom with all three threads blocked, so the model catches the bug class it is for. The suite checks that too, from a child process, because loom's deadlock panic aborts while its threads unwind.
- Limits: the queue is a counter here (its own Vyukov protocol needs a separate model), the spin phase is left out (it only re-checks the queue), and the bound is small. task-io's own implementation gets its own models under `cfg(loom)` (DIRECTIVES section 2).
- The ring now has its own model (`compare/models/tests/ring.rs`): 2 producers and 2 consumers, wrap-around through 2 cells, and refusal when full, with loom checking every cell access for a race; every value a push accepted comes out exactly once. Each thread does a fixed number of operations, because loom cannot bound a thread that spins until another makes progress (a first version that waited by yielding exceeded 100,000 branches per execution and ran for an hour).
- Finding: Vyukov's ring is wrong with 1 cell. A popped cell's sequence becomes `pos + mask + 1`, which with a mask of 0 equals a published cell's, so the next push overwrites a value that was never popped; loom reports the write racing the consumer's read, or the consumer spinning without end. The prototype's ring had no lower bound (its lanes are 4,096 to 65,536 cells, so it never hit this); it now clamps to 2, and task-io's lane capacity will be checked at build time to be at least 2 per ring.

**2026-11-11, `proto-wheel` (I-07), `compare/src/proto_wheel.rs`:**
- A hierarchical timing wheel (tokio's layout: 6 levels of 64 slots) whose `next_deadline` is the earliest live deadline exactly, not the start of the earliest occupied slot, so a timer thread built on it never wakes without a timer to fire. Lazy cancellation; a fixed capacity that counts cancelled entries not yet dropped, with a sweep when full, so insert-and-cancel churn cannot grow memory.
- The model test (an ordered map driven by random inserts, cancels, and advances over 600 seeds, with near and far deadlines and a full wheel) found two bugs in the first draft, both in timers beyond the wheel's span (64^6 ticks). Clamping a far deadline to `now + span - 1` can file it in the top level's current slot, which `advance` takes as due and files again in the same slot without end. And far timers filed at different times lose their order, so `next_deadline` reported a later timer than the earliest. Far timers now wait in a binary heap and move into the wheel when they come within its span; every timer in the wheel is then due before every timer in the heap. Removing the heap makes three of the model tests fail.
- I-07 run (the structures alone, single-threaded, 1,000,000 timers over 10 s in 1 µs ticks, half cancelled, time advanced in 1 ms steps; R1 under about 74% load from other sessions, so only same-run ratios mean anything): insert 16 ns against 9.3 ns for a binary heap push (pre-sizing the wheel's slab took it from 19.7 ns); drain 96 ns per fired timer against 418 ns. Insert plus drain, per timer: about 64 ns against 218 ns. On Linux (WSL, 100,000 timers): insert 12.8 ns against 8.3 ns, drain 54 ns against 97 ns.
- D-01 quick (100,000 jobs over 2 s, 4 workers, n=1): `proto-wheel` takes the heap scheduler's shape, one mutex taken on every submit. Windows: submit 54.7 ns against 37.4 ns (heap-scheduler) and 380 ns (tokio-timer); lateness p50 about 7.5 ms for all three, the condvar timeout's granularity (I-33). Linux: submit 590 ns against 415 ns and 361 ns, where the lock and the wake handoff, not the structure, are the cost; lateness p50 97 µs against 91 µs and 1.08 ms. No contender fired a job early or after its cancel.
- So the wheel wins on the timer thread and loses on submit while submit takes its lock. Next: I-43 (submits into per-submitter intakes, off the lock) and I-44 (cancel as one store). The cancel bar is a flag store: 0.15 to 0.21 ns per cancel for heap-scheduler, which never frees the entry; `proto-wheel` takes the lock and frees it (9 ns on Windows, 46 ns on Linux).

**2026-11-11, `proto-wheel-intake` (I-43), `compare/src/proto_intake.rs`:**

- loom 0.7.2 raised two false alarms on the way, both now avoided. A swap that reads 0, then a concurrent store of 1, then a second swap after joining the storing thread: loom let the second swap read 0 as well, which C11 forbids (the first swap read 0, so it is before the store in modification order, and the second swap happens after the store). And with the model's main thread as the timer, `thread::park` failed loom's internal `state.notified` assertion; the timer is now a spawned thread. Neither has been reported upstream yet.

- So preallocation is required (a growing file lost to SQLite at 1 submitter on Windows, probably because each flush also writes the file's new size; not checked), group commit carries the many-submitter rows (10 to 17 records per flush at 32 submitters with `sync_data`), and the better flush method depends on the platform: write-through on Windows NVMe, `sync_data` on WSL's virtual disk. That is I-46. The write-through rows rely on the device honouring forced-unit-access writes, which the documentation promises (POSIX `O_DSYNC`; Microsoft Learn, "CreateFileW") and only a power-loss test on the device can confirm; nothing here has been power-cut.
- I-28's test, P-01 with and without checksums: on Windows (one pass) 192 against 233 per second at 1 submitter and 2,562 against 3,044 at 32, slower with the checksum; on Linux 175 against 151 and 1,841 against 1,445, faster with it. The difference is the runs' noise. Measured apart (I-28 above), a checksum costs 2.6 to 4.4 ns at 64 bytes and 11 to 14 ns at 256 bytes; the fastest flush seen here took 110 us at p50, so the checksum is under 0.02% of P-01 latency, inside the 2% criterion. Verdict: adopted (the instruction where present, slicing-by-8 elsewhere), pending the gated run.
- On durability of the tail page: each flush rewrites the page that already holds acknowledged records. Their bytes are written unchanged, so a sector torn by a power loss holds either the old or the new copy of the same bytes, and the acknowledged records survive as long as single-sector writes are atomic (the NVMe atomic write unit is at least one logical block). The record after them may be torn, which the checksum detects and recovery truncates.
